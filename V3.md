# Compass v3 — Court Screens

v3 lives under `/v3` and is now the featured version on the home page. It shares v1's match
data and v2's court-stage tables, so nothing needs migrating — v2 keeps working side by side
at `/v2`. The v3 additions:

## Configurable match format

Setup no longer hard-codes the races. When a points-race format is picked, two new controls
appear:

- **Race target** — 9, 11, 16, 18, 21 or any custom number 4–99. *Race to N* means first to
  N points (sudden death at N-1 all). *Points total* means play stops at 2N-2 total points
  and whoever has more wins, with a sudden-death point if it's tied there.
- **Serve changes every** — 2, 3, 4, 5 or any custom number 1–10 of points before the serve
  changes hands. The serve pips on every screen size themselves to match.

Both are stored on the tournament (`raceTarget`, `serveEvery` — run `npx prisma db push`
once to add the columns). `0` means "not configured", which keeps the historical defaults
(9-point total / first to 16 / serve every 4), so tournaments seeded before this upgrade
score exactly as before.

Every screen derives its wording from the same helpers in `src/lib/types.ts`
(`raceTargetOf`, `serveEveryOf`, `matchFormatLabel`), so the setup page, the TVs, the big
board, the coach phones and the score editor can never disagree about the rules.

## Americano

A social format where **partners rotate every round**. Pick it in setup and enter
individuals rather than pairs — the app makes up the teams. Every round each player gets a
new partner and a new pair of opponents, and each player carries their **own** points total
all evening. The winner is the highest individual scorer; nobody is knocked out.

- **The rotation is solved, not guessed.** Partnerships come from the circle method (a
  1-factorization of the complete graph), so for any run up to the natural length of the
  format — `players − 1` rounds — *no two people are ever made to partner twice*. Opponents
  are then spread greedily over those fixed pairs. With a player count that isn't a multiple
  of four, the byes rotate so everyone sits out within one of everyone else.
- **Rounds are played in order.** Every match is generated up front, but only the current
  round is playable; finishing it opens the next. Without that the court queue would start a
  round-five match the moment those four were free, and the round a player is told to watch
  for would not match what is on court.
- **Setup previews the actual draw** — every round, who partners whom, who sits out, and a
  quality line ("14 matches · 7 each · nobody repeats a partner"). It comes from the same
  generator the seeder uses, so what you read is what you get.
- **Scoring is per person.** Standings, the podium and the ceremony all rank individuals on
  points won, and the court screens show pairings ("Ana & Ben") wherever a side is drawn. The
  player card tells you who you are **with** this round, not just who you are against.

Set the race target and serve rotation as normal — an americano is usually a short race to
16 or 21.

## Mexicano

The same idea played competitively: individuals, rotating partners, personal points totals —
but the pairings are **drawn from the standings** instead of fixed in advance. Each round the
players are ranked on points and taken four at a time; within each four the leader partners
the fourth against the second and third. So the top four meet on the first court, the next
four on the next, and winning moves you up into tougher company.

- **Rounds are built as you go.** Only round 1 can be known at seeding time (it comes off the
  entry order, so enter players strongest first), and each later round is generated when the
  previous one finishes. Setup previews round 1 only and says so — showing a full schedule
  would be inventing one.
- **Repeat partners are not avoided,** unlike the americano. Partnering the same person twice
  means you are both still near the same place on the table; "fixing" that would quietly turn
  the draw back into an americano.
- **Byes rotate on byes taken, not on standing** — resting the bottom of the table every round
  would mean the people having the worst night also play the least.
- **Undo takes the next round with it.** That round was derived from a table the undo just
  changed, so keeping it would show pairings the standings no longer justify. It refuses
  outright once a later round has actually started.

## King of the court

A ladder of courts, climbed two players at a time. The courts are ranked — court 1 is the
**king court** — and every round each one plays its own 2 v 2. Then the two winners of each
court move **up** a rung and the two losers move **down**, so the standard on each court
sorts itself out over the evening and the last round on the king court is the closest thing
the night has to a final.

Nothing here is global, which is what makes it a different format rather than a variation:
you move relative to the people you just played, so a player can climb all night without
ever topping the points table.

- **The boundaries hold.** Winners on the king court have nowhere to go, so they stay; losers
  on the bottom court likewise. Every court therefore takes exactly two players from each
  direction.
- **Partners come from opposite ends.** The two arrivals from above are split across the net
  from the two from below, so the pair that just won together never goes straight back out as
  the same team.
- **The field must divide into fours**, at least 8. A rung with three players on it cannot
  play, and a floating bench would break the promotion and relegation that is the whole point.
  Setup says so rather than quietly dropping someone.
- **The rung is on the screen**: matches read "Round 3 · King court" / "Round 3 · Court 2",
  so a player always knows which one they are on. Setup previews the opening ladder.

## Team americano

Two fixed sides, one scoreboard each. Enter one team and then the other — the first half of
the list is Team A, the rest Team B. Every round you partner someone else **from your own
team** and play two from the other side, and every point you win is added to your **team's**
total. The individual rotation is an americano; the result that matters is a single number
per team.

It is the format for "our club against yours", where people want to rotate partners all
evening and still have a side to belong to.

- **Partners come from your team, opponents never do.** The partner rotation inside each
  team is the same circle method the plain americano uses, so nobody repeats a team-mate
  until the team runs out of combinations. The pairs are then offset each round so the same
  two pairs don't meet all night.
- **The default round count is the team's natural length** — a team of four has three
  rotations, not eight — so the default schedule never contains a repeat. Ask for more rounds
  and setup tells you how many repeats that buys.
- **The field must divide into fours**, at least 8: two equal teams that each split into pairs.
- **The court screens show both tables** — the team totals that decide it, with the individual
  scorers beside them, since people still want to see who is winning the points for their side.
  The podium and ceremony award **teams**, not people.

## Mixicano

Every pair is one player from each of two groups. Enter two equal groups — in a mixed session
that is everyone from one side of the draw and then the other — and a pair is always one from
each. You get a new partner from the opposite group every round, so over a full run you play
once with everybody there rather than once with half of them. Scoring is individual, exactly
as in a plain americano.

The contrast with the team americano is the point of having both: there your partner comes
from **your** side and the points belong to that side; here your partner comes from the
**other** group and the points are your own. One format is about belonging to a team, the
other about mixing across a division that is not a team at all.

- **The partner rotation is a Latin square** — group A's players step round group B's — so over
  `groupSize` rounds every cross-group pairing happens exactly once and none happens twice.
  The resulting pairs are then matched by the circle method, so opponents vary too.
- **Groups get one more round than a team americano**: pairing across a divide has no "you
  cannot partner yourself" to lose, so groups of four give four clean rounds rather than three.
- **The field must divide into fours**, minimum 4 — two equal groups that make whole matches.

## Winner court

One court and a queue. The pair that **wins stays on** — keeping the court and their
partnership — the pair that loses goes to the back of the line, and the next two waiting come
on to challenge. The oldest social format there is, and the one people mean by "winners stay
on".

The difference from king of the court is what happens to you when you win. There, winning
moves you up a rung and breaks your pair up to be re-drawn. Here winning changes nothing: you
keep the court and your partner, and the reward for a run of wins is that you never leave.
Losing is what moves you, and it moves you to the back of a queue rather than down a ladder.

- **The queue is never stored.** It is replayed from the results so far, which is the only way
  it can still be right after an undo — a stored queue would keep the order produced by a
  result that no longer exists, and the wrong two people would be called on.
- **One match at a time**, so the format uses a single court however many you have selected.
  Setup says so.
- **At least 6 players**: four on court and a pair waiting, or there is no queue at all. The
  count need not be even — the queue neither grows nor shrinks, since two join the back for
  every two called off the front.
- **The court screens show the streak** — "Holding the court · 3 wins in a row" — because that
  is the thing everyone in the queue is watching.

## Mixed mexicano

Both at once: every pair is one player from each group, **and** each round is redrawn from the
standings. Neither parent format gives you both — a mexicano ranks the whole field as one list
and will happily pair two players from the same group, and a mixicano's rotation is fixed in
advance and takes no notice of who is winning.

Each group is ranked **on its own**, so the top two of each meet on the first court, the next
two of each on the second, and so on down. Inside every four the pairs cross both the groups
and the ranks — group A's better player partners group B's weaker one — which is the same
balancing idea as the mexicano's "leader partners the fourth", expressed under the constraint
that a pair must have one from each side.

- **Ranking the groups separately is what keeps it honest.** It means "the top court" holds the
  best two of each group, which is a thing a player can see and aim at. Ranking everyone as one
  list and then repairing the pairs afterwards would put people there for reasons they could
  not follow.
- **The court screens show both group tables**, because those two tables are literally how the
  next round gets drawn.
- **The field must divide into fours** — two equal groups that make whole matches.

## Mixed americano

A plain americano rotation over the whole field, with the two groups **ranked separately** so
each has its own winner. Partners are drawn from everyone — you will often partner someone
from your own group — and the groups exist only for the tables and the medals.

**This is not the mixicano**, and the setup screen says so where you choose. The two are easy
to confuse and are the two sensible readings of "mixed":

| | Pairs | Rotation | Ranking |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mixicano | always one from each group | fixed in advance | one list |
| Mixed americano | anyone with anyone | fixed in advance | one table per group |

Pick this one for a mixed event where you want a winner from each group but do not want to
force every pair to be mixed; pick the mixicano when the mixed pairing is the point.

- **The rotation is exactly the plain americano's** — the same circle method, so nobody repeats
  a partner — because the groups change how it is read, never how it is drawn.
- **The field needs an even number of players**, at least 4, so the two groups come out equal.
  It does not need a multiple of four: nothing here requires a pair to come from a particular
  group.
- **The podium names the group** ("Group A · 71 points"), so the ceremony reads correctly at a
  mixed event while still crowning one overall champion.

## Mixed team americano

The team americano with every pair **mixed within its own side**. Two fixed teams, each split
into two halves; you always partner someone from the other half of *your* team, always play
the other team, and every point you win goes to your team's total. At a mixed club event this
is what "our team against yours" usually means.

Enter it in **quarters**: team A's first half, team A's second half, then team B's two halves.
Setup lays the four out so you can check them before starting.

- **It is the only format needing two divisions per player**, and they do different jobs:
  which side you score for, and which half of it you may partner across. Every other format
  needs one or the other, never both, so this one gets its own `pairGroup` column rather than
  encoding two things in one number.
- **The partner rotation is the mixicano's Latin square**, applied inside each team — one half
  steps round the other — so over `half` rounds you partner everyone on the far side of your
  own team exactly once and never anyone from your own half.
- **Scoring and medals are the team americano's**: team totals decide it, the individual
  scorers are shown beside them, and the podium awards teams.
- **The field must divide into fours**, at least 8: two teams that each split into halves.

## Adding a format

Every format declares itself once, in `src/lib/bracket/formats.ts` — its name, its blurb, what
a legal field looks like, how many rounds it defaults to, the line under the standings, and
which behaviours it shares with others. The setup form, the setup API, the seeder, the
standings tables and the idle-screen stats all read from that table, so a format cannot be
half-added: it either declares a behaviour or it does not have it.

That registry exists because the alternative was tried. Twelve formats had grown across seven
files, each with its own `format === "..."` chain, and forgetting one was silent — the format
simply behaved like whatever the fallthrough happened to be. It produced the same
default-rounds bug twice and, in one edit, quietly relabelled a different format's stats card.

To add a thirteenth:

1. Write its schedule (or its round-generation rule) in its own module under `lib/bracket/`.
2. Add one entry to `FORMATS`, choosing a family for the setup screen.
3. Teach the seeder how to lay out round 1, and — if rounds are derived — `rotatingRounds.ts`
   how to build the next one. These are the only two places that genuinely differ per format.
4. Run `npx tsx scripts/checkFormats.ts`. It fails if the registry and the predicates disagree,
   if a validator accepts a field its generator would throw on, if a default round count would
   schedule a repeated partner, or if a display label is missing.

## Match point & sudden death

`src/lib/scoring/pressure.ts` asks the engine itself whether the next point can end the
match — each side's hypothetical point is played into a copy of the state and the answer is
read off the result. That makes it correct for every format, including the points-total
quirk where the trailing side's point ends the match but the leader wins.

- The court TV shows a breathing **MATCH POINT · name** ribbon, or a red
  **SUDDEN DEATH · next point wins** when either side's point would take it.
- The coach console shows the same as a chip on the tap zone it applies to.

Sudden death is deliberately stricter than "the next point ends it": under the points-total
rule the final point is match point for the leader whoever scores it, not sudden death.

## Other v3 upgrades

- **Race progress bars** under each side on the live scoreboard, filling toward the target.
  Drawn with a CSS transition rather than a JS animation, so a backgrounded TV tab can never
  show a stale width.
- **Score glow** — a soft pulse of light behind the number each time a side scores.
- **Haptics** on the coach console: a light tick per point, a longer buzz for game/set, a
  triple buzz on the match-winning tap.
- **Fixes**: the v3 awards remote posted to `/api/v3/ceremony`, which does not exist (the
  presentation could never start from `/v3/ceremony`); it now posts to the shared v2
  endpoint. The TV's "back to v2 hub" copy is also gone.

## Saved entrant lists

Resetting a draw deletes the players, which is exactly the moment an organiser re-running the
same group does not want to retype fourteen names. The setup screen keeps named lists — "Save
current" stores whoever is typed in, "Load" puts them back, and saving under an existing name
replaces it, so save doubles as update.

They live in their own table, deliberately unattached to any tournament, so a full reset leaves
them alone. Loading into a compass draw pads or trims to its sixteen rows and says so.

## Finishing a race: sudden death or deuce

"Race to N" offers two endings, chosen in setup:

- **Sudden death** (the default, and how it always behaved): reaching N wins it, so N-1 all is
  decided by the next point.
- **Win by two (deuce)**: the race behaves like a tiebreak and runs on past N until someone
  leads by two — 17-15, 22-20, with no cap.

Only "first to N" offers this. The points-total rule already ends on a fixed total, so a margin
requirement has nothing to bite on, and setup hides the choice there.

Hand-entered scores follow the same rule: past the target the margin must be exactly two, since
the race stops the moment someone is two clear.

## Sets and games in the rotating formats

Choosing a rotating format sets a short points race, because that is how they are normally
played — but it is only a default. Pick any set-based option underneath and the best-of controls
appear; americano, mexicano and the rest will happily run as sets. The setup screen says so
rather than leaving it to be discovered.

## Before deploying

The formats added since v3 need columns the live database may not have yet
(`TournamentConfig.amRounds`, `Player.team`, `Player.pairGroup`, and the two partner columns on
`Match`). The app cannot run without them — every query touching the tournament config fails,
not just the new formats.

Check what a database is missing, changing nothing:

```bash
npx prisma migrate diff --from-url "$POSTGRES_URL_NON_POOLING" --to-schema-datamodel prisma/schema.prisma --script
```

The change is additive — new columns, all defaulted or nullable, plus two foreign keys — so it
does not touch an event already in progress. Apply it with `npx prisma db push` against that
database. Note that the Prisma CLI reads `.env`, not `.env.local`, so source the latter first
if that is where the credentials live.

## A safe local dev database

`vercel env pull` puts the **production** Neon credentials in `.env.local`, and
`src/lib/db.ts` refuses to let `npm run dev` touch that database. The dev loop instead runs
on PGlite (Postgres in WASM, zero install):

```bash
npm run dev:db          # terminal 1 — local Postgres on 127.0.0.1:5433, data in .pglite-dev/
npm run dev:local:push  # once — create the tables
npm run dev:local       # terminal 2 — dev server pointed at the local DB
```

Delete `.pglite-dev/` for a clean slate. Seeding, resetting and simulating are all harmless
here. (`ALLOW_PROD_DB_IN_DEV=1 npm run dev` remains the explicit opt-in for live data.)

## Checks

Everything at once:

```bash
npm run check       # every logic suite — fast, no database
npm run check:db    # the database flows as well, one fresh database each
```

`check:db` starts and stops its own database between flows, because PGlite serves one shared
session and a full flow leaves it unusable for the next client. Stop your own `npm run dev:db`
(and any dev server using it) first — the runner says so rather than failing halfway through.

Or one at a time:

```bash
npx tsx scripts/checkFormats.ts       # the format registry: rules, defaults and labels all coherent
npx tsx scripts/checkRace16.ts        # the historical race rules, unchanged
npx tsx scripts/checkFlexRace.ts      # configurable targets, serve rotation, match point/sudden death
npx tsx scripts/checkAmericano.ts     # the rotation: no repeated partners, even rest, no double-booking
npx tsx scripts/checkAmericanoFlow.ts # americano end to end against the local dev database
npx tsx scripts/checkMexicano.ts      # mexicano: rounds drawn from the standings, byes, undo
npx tsx scripts/checkKingCourt.ts     # the ladder as pure logic: promotion, relegation, boundaries
npx tsx scripts/checkKingCourtFlow.ts # king of the court end to end against the local dev database
npx tsx scripts/checkTeamAmericano.ts     # team schedule: partners in-team, opponents cross-team
npx tsx scripts/checkTeamAmericanoFlow.ts # team americano end to end against the local dev database
npx tsx scripts/checkMixicano.ts          # mixicano schedule: every pair crosses the two groups
npx tsx scripts/checkMixicanoFlow.ts      # mixicano end to end against the local dev database
npx tsx scripts/checkWinnerCourt.ts       # the queue: winners hold, losers go to the back
npx tsx scripts/checkWinnerCourtFlow.ts   # winner court end to end against the local dev database
npx tsx scripts/checkMixedMexicano.ts     # mixed mexicano: courts by group rank, pairs across groups
npx tsx scripts/checkMixedMexicanoFlow.ts # mixed mexicano end to end against the local dev database
npx tsx scripts/checkMixedAmericanoFlow.ts # mixed americano: free rotation, per-group tables
npx tsx scripts/checkMixedTeamAmericano.ts     # mixed team schedule: partners cross your team's halves
npx tsx scripts/checkMixedTeamAmericanoFlow.ts # mixed team americano end to end against the dev database
npx tsx scripts/checkDecider.ts       # round-robin play-off
npx tsx scripts/checkTwoGroup.ts      # two groups → semis → final
```

The `*Flow` scripts and `checkMexicano` talk to a real database. Start `npm run dev:db`, stop the dev
server while they run, and **restart the database between runs** — PGlite serves one shared
session and a full run leaves it unusable for the next client. Nothing in `.pglite-dev/` is
lost by restarting.
